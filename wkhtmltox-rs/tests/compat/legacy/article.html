The Hidden Cost of Technical Debt: Lessons from a Decade of Legacy System Migrations

When organisations delay modernisation, the bill arrives eventually — with interest. A look at why technical debt accumulates, how it manifests, and what successful migration programmes share in common.

By Dr. Elena Marchetti • Senior Contributing Editor, Enterprise Technology Review • Published 4 November 2024 • Revised 18 November 2024

In the spring of 2019, a major European retail bank quietly allocated €340 million to a programme that appeared nowhere in its annual report under the heading "Innovation" or "Digital Transformation." The budget was listed, almost apologetically, under "Infrastructure Renewal." What the bank was paying for was the removal of a COBOL-based core banking platform that had first gone live in 1987 — the year the Soviet Union still had nine years to run and the World Wide Web had not yet been imagined by Tim Berners-Lee. The system had accrued 32 years of patches, workarounds, undocumented dependencies, and what engineers on the project described, in the understated vocabulary of the profession, as "accumulated complexity."

The bank's situation is far from unusual. Across industries — from healthcare and government to logistics and financial services — organisations are grappling with the compounding consequences of technical decisions made decades ago. The phenomenon has a name — technical debt — coined by the software engineer Ward Cunningham in 1992 as an analogy for the short-term borrowing of implementation quality in exchange for speed of delivery. Like financial debt, technical debt accumulates interest: the longer it goes unaddressed, the more expensive it becomes to service and ultimately to repay.

What makes the current moment distinctive is the pace at which the interest rate has risen. Cloud architectures, API-first integration paradigms, real-time data expectations, and regulatory mandates such as the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and the NIS2 Directive have collectively changed the cost function. Systems that were "good enough" in 2005 are no longer adequate in 2024, not because they have degraded but because the world around them has transformed.

Why Technical Debt Accumulates

The mechanism by which technical debt accumulates is well understood in theory, yet remarkably difficult to prevent in practice. At the root lies a structural misalignment between the time horizons of those who bear the cost of deferred maintenance and those who benefit from the short-term savings. A business unit that presses for a feature to be shipped ahead of schedule will almost never be the same team that inherits the integration complexity three years later. A CFO who approves a budget that excludes infrastructure uplift is unlikely still to hold the role when the resulting outage materialises.

This is compounded by what researchers at MIT Sloan have termed "the visibility gap": the true state of a software system is opaque to non-technical stakeholders in a way that, say, a deteriorating building roof is not. A CFO who walks past peeling paint understands intuitively that maintenance is overdue; the same CFO reviewing a dashboard of application uptime metrics may have no insight into the fact that the system supporting those numbers is held together by logic that no living engineer fully understands and that a single unhandled edge case could bring down.

Organisational dynamics reinforce the problem. Engineering teams that flag systemic risk are sometimes perceived as obstructive or overly conservative. Budget cycles that operate on annual horizons are poorly matched to infrastructure problems that mature over five to ten years. And the very success of legacy systems — their proven reliability under known conditions — creates a powerful institutional argument against touching them. "If it ain't broke, don't fix it" is engineering folklore elevated to corporate policy in many large organisations.

"We knew for at least six years that the platform needed replacing. Every year, the engineering team produced a risk register that flagged it as critical. Every year, the capital expenditure request was deferred in favour of customer-facing features that could be tied to revenue projections. The eventual migration cost was roughly four times what it would have been had we acted when the problem was first clearly identified." — Chief Technology Officer, European retail banking group (name withheld)

The insight embedded in this account — that the cost of inaction is a multiple of the cost of timely action — is widely reported by practitioners but difficult to quantify prospectively. This is the central challenge of the technical debt conversation: the counterfactual is unknowable. No organisation can point to the migration it did not run and state with precision what it would have cost in 2018 compared to what it cost in 2024. What can be measured, with increasing rigour, is the total cost of delay once the migration is underway.

The cost of inaction is a multiple of the cost of timely action — yet the counterfactual is always unknowable until it is too late.

The Anatomy of a Migration Programme

Over the past decade, a sufficient number of large-scale legacy migrations have been completed — and documented, often painfully — to allow some pattern recognition about what distinguishes those that succeed from those that fail or overrun. The evidence is not encouraging: a 2023 survey by McKinsey Digital found that 70% of large technology transformation programmes fail to meet their original scope, schedule, or budget targets. A report by the UK National Audit Office covering government digital programmes over the same period found an average cost overrun of 44%.

The failures share certain characteristics. They typically begin with an underestimation of system complexity, often because the documentation of the legacy system is incomplete or inaccurate, and the institutional knowledge of how it actually behaves in production is distributed across the minds of engineers who have since retired or moved on. The Royal Bank of Scotland's failed core banking migration in 2012, which resulted in a six-day outage affecting 6.5 million customers, was attributed in part to the fact that the system being replaced contained code that no one in the organisation could confidently describe.

A second characteristic of failed migrations is the adoption of a "big bang" cutover strategy: the attempt to replace the old system entirely at a single moment in time. The appeal is understandable. Running two systems in parallel is expensive and operationally complex. But the big bang approach concentrates risk into a single irreversible event, and when things go wrong — as they frequently do — there is no graceful fallback. The data may have been migrated; the old system may have been decommissioned; the only path forward is through.

Successful programmes, by contrast, almost universally adopt a strangler fig pattern or equivalent incremental approach, replacing the legacy system one bounded context or service at a time while the remainder continues to operate. Martin Fowler, who popularised the term after the parasitic plant that gradually surrounds and replaces its host tree, documented this approach in the early 2000s; it has since become a foundational principle of migration engineering.

The Australian Tax Office's migration of its enterprise core away from a thirty-year-old mainframe platform — completed in stages between 2014 and 2021 — is frequently cited as a reference implementation of the approach. The programme involved 18 staged releases, a parallel-run period of 26 months for the most critical workloads, and an investment in automated regression testing that, at its peak, executed over 400,000 test cases per nightly build. The total cost exceeded AUD 1 billion over seven years, but the programme delivered on schedule and without a single production incident caused by the migration itself — a record regarded as exceptional in the field.

What the Numbers Do and Do Not Tell Us

The difficulty with technical debt as a management concept is that it resists precise quantification at exactly the moment when precision matters most: when the case for investment must be made to a board or a finance committee. Qualitative descriptions of risk and complexity do not translate naturally into the language of capital expenditure requests and net present value calculations.

Several frameworks have been developed to address this. SQALE (Software Quality Assessment based on Lifecycle Expectations), developed by Jean-Louis Letouzey, attempts to assign a remediation cost in person-hours to each defect or complexity issue identified by static analysis tools, and to aggregate these into a total debt figure. Tools such as SonarQube implement variants of this approach and produce dashboards that express technical debt in time units. The appeal is obvious: a CTO who can say "our technical debt is equivalent to 42 developer-years of remediation effort" has a number to put in a presentation.

The limitation is equally obvious. Static analysis tools measure what they can measure: code complexity, duplication, test coverage, known vulnerability patterns. They do not measure architectural coupling, undocumented runtime dependencies, the fragility of integrations that have never been formally tested end-to-end, or the organisational knowledge debt that accumulates when the engineers who understand a system's quirks are replaced by engineers who are trained to avoid touching them. The number on the dashboard is real, but it represents the measurable fraction of a larger and less tractable whole.

This is why the most rigorous practitioners of technical debt management increasingly combine quantitative metrics with qualitative risk assessment, embedding engineers with explicit architectural review responsibilities and establishing regular ceremonies — often quarterly or semi-annual "architecture health" reviews — at which the state of the estate is examined by people with both technical and commercial context. The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty but to ensure that decisions about where to invest are made with as complete a picture as the available information permits.

"The metric you choose to measure technical debt shapes the conversations you have about it. If you measure lines of code, you'll have conversations about lines of code. If you measure the probability that a given system will require emergency intervention in the next twelve months, you'll have conversations about operational risk and business continuity. Both are true; only one is actionable at the board level." — Dr. Nicole Forsgren, co-author of Accelerate: The Science of Lean Software and DevOps

The coming years are likely to force a reckoning for many organisations that have deferred this reckoning. The convergence of regulatory pressure, the maturation of AI-assisted code analysis and migration tooling, and the growing body of empirical evidence about the economics of technical debt is gradually shifting the conversation from "should we do this?" to "when and how?" The organisations that engage with that question deliberately, armed with honest assessments of their current state and realistic plans for how to evolve it, will be better positioned than those that wait for a production incident or a regulatory notice to make the decision for them.

The retail bank mentioned at the opening of this article completed its core banking migration in August 2023, two months ahead of its revised schedule and €18 million under its final budget. The engineers who led the programme attribute the outcome to a decision made early in the process: to treat the migration not as a technology project but as an organisational transformation, with all the investment in change management, communication, and stakeholder alignment that implied. The technical challenges, they note, were substantial but tractable. The human challenges were the ones that nearly derailed it.
